Enjoy the moment now and check the time later

Kairos is a silent phone companion for soldiers. It stays completely quiet while your phone is put away, and when you come back it asks whether that time was worth keeping. You won't see a clock until the end of the day, when you finally find out how long it really was.

The idea

Flexible time

I was thinking about flexible time, the idea that the same hour can feel long or short depending on the moment. For me, time was most flexible when I served as a soldier in Korea.

Night watch

Standing guard alone in the dark, I checked my Casio watch almost every minute, and every minute felt endless. When I tried counting seconds in my head, I was always sure more time had gone by than really had.

Dinner with the squad

Then there were the moments that flew by without me even noticing, like dinner with my squad after drills. Those were the moments that actually mattered, and they disappeared without a trace.

So, Kairos

In Greek, chronos is clock time and kairos is the right moment. Kairos protects your time away from the phone, helps you keep the moments that mattered, and only shows the clock afterward.

Research

What it builds on

Chronos vs. kairos

Ancient Greek has two words for time: chronos, measured clock time, and kairos, the meaningful or "right" moment. The app is named after the second one.

Monochronic & polychronic time

Anthropologist Edward T. Hall described cultures that schedule one thing at a time by the clock, and cultures that treat time as flexible and put people first. Military life is extremely monochronic; Kairos makes room for the other kind.

Mind time vs. clock time

Claudia Hammond's Time Warped explains how fear, boredom and waiting stretch time, and how we judge time differently looking forward than looking back (the "Holiday Paradox"). That's why Kairos asks first and reveals the clock later.

Interviews

Five people (a college friend, a roommate, someone at the gym, someone at a coffee shop and my mom) described time as deadlines, routine, progress, mood and memories. My own military experience became the core story.

Process

Iterations & how I used AI

v1 · The Day Grows Back

My first prototype was a number-free clock: a dial of grass, one blade per minute, mowed by a little gardener. It worked, but my instructor pushed me to research "flexible time" further and try a more different route.

Three routes → Kairos

I compared an elastic planner, a "kairos" app that only shows meaningful moments, and a shared flexible-time app. I picked the second, then reshaped it around my military story: silent while the phone is away, with the clock revealed at night.

Classmate feedback

  • Use gradients and fill empty space → added topographic map textures and a sealed letter
  • Strengthen core features before adding more → cut voice notes and custom tags, and focused on "felt vs. real"
  • Try a social angle → a private unit board, dog-tag QR and squad profiles

How I used AI

  • Claude (Anthropic) helped me put my moodboard and concept into words, write interview questions, and draft and edit my presentation scripts. The military story and the opinions in it are my own.
  • I designed the prototypes by prompting Claude in Claude Design, giving feedback screen by screen ("too many words", "make the grass look like grass", "military colors", "add a letter behind sealed").
  • Claude searched for research sources (Hall, Hammond, chronos/kairos), which I read and chose from.
  • Claude wrote the code that turns the design screens into this static demo, took the screenshots, recorded the walkthrough and set up this repository.
  • The direction was mine: the silent lock-screen note, sealing a moment until night, the soldier focus, and the squad features.

What I learned

AI makes it fast to build a lot, but my instructor was right that speed isn't the point. The project only got interesting once I brought in something personal: counting minutes on night watch. The best prompts were specific reactions to what I saw, not requests for more features.
